# Screenplay Development Workflow

Use this reference when expanding an unwritten installment from author-supplied plot points while preserving existing continuity and the author's established screenplay style.

## 1. Lock scope before plotting

Record the installment's allowed viewpoint, characters, locations, time plane, runtime target, opening state, and endpoint. Treat exclusions as hard boundaries: a single-character subjective episode must not gain external cutaways merely because unresolved action exists outside it.

Separate inputs into:

- **Inherited continuity:** facts already established by written and produced sources.
- **Author-supplied working locks:** decisions approved for the new screenplay.
- **Open decisions:** mechanics or motives still awaiting author choice.
- **Proposals:** optional ideas that must not enter canon or durable recall as facts.

When the author asks to change foundations before moving to structure, stop at the architecture layer. Do not continue into a beat sheet until the changes are accepted.

## 2. Define the dramatic engine

Write one causal sentence containing:

1. What the protagonist wants.
2. What the apparent guide or antagonist offers.
3. What the guide secretly wants.
4. What revelation reverses the relationship.
5. What choice proves the protagonist's change.

Every major set piece must advance at least two of plot, character, theme, mystery, or capability. Remove attractive scenes that only demonstrate spectacle.

## 3. Build the protagonist's belief arc

State four points explicitly:

- **Starting belief:** what the protagonist currently uses to understand themself.
- **Challenge:** what new evidence makes that belief insufficient.
- **Crisis:** how the antagonist weaponizes that uncertainty.
- **Resolution:** what the protagonist chooses without requiring every mystery to be solved.

Do not rewrite the starting belief into a fear the character has never shown. Ground it in existing dialogue and behavior, then let the episode create the new doubt.

## 4. Motivate teaching and training sequences

A mentor or antagonist must have both a plausible visible reason and a hidden strategic reason to grant skills.

For each lesson, record:

- What the teacher tells the protagonist the exercise will diagnose or teach.
- What the teacher is secretly testing.
- Why this specific skill is the best test rather than an arbitrary cool ability.
- What practical capability remains afterward.
- What thematic doubt or emotional residue the experience creates.
- How the skill pays off in the climax.

Use a diagnostic ladder for escalating tests:

1. **Baseline integration:** a controlled external skill tests whether foreign procedural memory can be retained.
2. **Embodied adaptation:** a physical skill tests whether borrowed experience can be adapted to a different body.
3. **Stress revelation:** pressure exposes a latent ability the teacher is actually searching for.
4. **Climactic recombination:** the protagonist uses borrowed skills through personal judgment, proving they are more than the installed material.

A lesson is not justified by “the character needs this later.” Its in-scene giver must have a reason to teach it now.

## 5. Pay off clues through exact wording

When a prior message or riddle drives the episode, list what it literally promises and what it does not. Favor a payoff that is exact but initially misunderstood. A hostile figure can “help” find an answer inadvertently; the clue need not promise safety, alliance, or willing assistance.

Tie the clue to a causal chain:

- Why the messenger knew the destination.
- Why the protagonist could not receive a direct answer.
- Why this particular person or system can expose the truth.
- What part of the messenger's prior knowledge remains a later mystery.

Avoid making the messenger omniscient merely to close a plot gap. Reveal the functional reason for the direction while reserving how the messenger acquired that knowledge when the larger mythology needs it.

## 6. Reveal answers and hints at different depths

For a mystery episode, divide discoveries into:

- **Confirmed this episode:** the promised identity, power, or relationship reveal.
- **Diagnostic hints:** a protected gap, abnormal architecture, incompatible origin pattern, or latent capability.
- **Reserved mythology:** creator, full transformation mechanism, faction history, or complete motive.

A hint must narrow possibilities. Repeating “something is unusual” without a new constraint is not progress. Conversely, do not answer the series-level mystery merely because a character has database access.

## 7. Lock power mechanics before action structure

For each ability define:

- Trigger.
- Observable effect.
- Target and range.
- Duration and cost.
- Whether it affects the user.
- What it cannot do.
- How it behaves in physical versus subjective or digital space.
- Why it solves the climax without permanently solving future conflicts.

Treat production feasibility as a design constraint early. Remove or defer an ability when it is disproportionately difficult to depict and another locked ability can carry the same dramatic function.

Make the reveal pay off immediately: if an ability identifies the protagonist as the sought-after key, the antagonist's objective should reverse at that moment, and the protagonist should later use that same ability to escape.

### Power-to-choreography revision checklist

When a character's governing power changes after action has been written:

1. Separate the approved mechanism, its applications, and its delivery methods. A shared biological basis can explain both tissue repair and plant growth without making healing plant-mediated. Treat suggested costs and limits as proposals until approved; do not generalize one scene's exhaustion into an invented universal rule.
2. Read the character entry and search the entire named screenplay for the character, weapon aliases, old ability nouns, aftermath, restraints, injuries, and rescue callbacks. Read neighboring passages before replacing matches; downstream dependencies often survive beyond the first fight.
3. Preserve the encounter's outcome ledger: who is injured, what opens the next attack, which character earns the counter, and who retains the contested object. Keep approved cutting choreography when the power changes unless the author also changes the weapon.
4. Translate each retained power use into a visible causal chain: existing source, activation, growth or repair, physical effect, and cost. For growth-based abilities, establish living material and resources before use; show anchored growth changing shape rather than implying creation from bare metal or telekinetic motion of detached matter.
5. Set up rescues before impact. Grow or place a support while the character has time, then let it bend or break under the falling weight; an unexplained last-second tendril can accidentally grant reach, speed, or control the approved mechanism lacks.
6. Repair small staging dependencies with ordinary actions where possible. A cargo strap can replace a magical restraint, but explicitly release it before the restrained character exits; replacing a noun alone can leave the next action impossible.
7. Apply bounded exact replacements and compare the saved file with the expected transformed original. Confirm unaffected text and sibling options are unchanged, read each revised passage with its neighbors, and search for obsolete weapon names and aftermath references.
8. Update the governing character entry, current index and memory together. Rewrite current summaries instead of stacking contradictory 'latest' notes. Mark historical directives superseded and replace stale composite roster facts while preserving their unaffected entries, because a new correction does not prevent an older roster from being retrieved.

## 8. Construct the reveal ladder

Reveal identity and motive through stages:

1. Benign explanation.
2. Behavior that does not fit the explanation.
3. Environmental evidence.
4. The protagonist's deduction.
5. Confirmation by the revealed character.
6. Consequence that changes the objective.

Avoid a sudden villain monologue. Plant lines and physical behavior that acquire a second meaning after the reveal.

### Prepare a later reveal without leaking it early

When a scene exists to make a revelation in a later installment more emotional, separate three layers before writing it:

- **Establish now:** the relationship, personality, remembered behavior, or emotional bond the audience must understand.
- **Recontextualize later:** the hidden survival, allegiance, leadership role, betrayal, or causal involvement that will change the scene's meaning.
- **Do not imply yet:** reactions, present-day images, faction symbols, knowing dialogue, or suspicious behavior that would prematurely expose the connection.

Do not add a recognition beat merely because a present character knows the hidden truth. If the author reserves that connection, the knowledgeable character must behave neutrally unless a deliberate clue has been approved. Build the future impact through the protagonist's genuine attachment, not through an accidental teaser.

For a character-building memory, dramatize traits through behavior rather than labels. Establish intelligence by letting the character notice, test, revise, or accept a better answer. Establish awkward affection through attention, practical help, trust, inclusion, or a hesitant gesture. When contrasting caregivers, give each a distinct love language instead of making one warmer only by making the other uncaring.

Place the memory according to its dramatic job. If it is an emotional anchor after a sequence of lessons, complete every required lesson first, then use the memory as contrast and as later leverage. After moving it, retime the full treatment, renumber scenes sequentially, update transitions and progression summaries, and re-run the required-field/count validator; a localized insertion can otherwise leave the architecture internally contradictory.

## 9. Build the treatment, then pages

After architecture is approved:

1. Create a timed sequence for the target runtime.
2. Give every sequence an objective, reversal, reveal, and exit condition.
3. Track which working lock each sequence pays off.
4. Verify the climax uses capabilities earned earlier.
5. Verify the ending changes the protagonist while preserving reserved mysteries.
6. Only then draft screenplay pages in the author's observed style.

Before imitating style, inspect at least two adjacent screenplays across openings, dialogue scenes, action sequences, and endings. Match concrete habits—scene-heading format, action-paragraph length, visual density, parenthetical use, pacing devices, and cliffhanger shape—without copying source dialogue.

## 10. Revise working locks cleanly

When the author changes a term, mechanic, lesson, or motive:

- Edit the working architecture rather than appending contradictory notes.
- Preserve the author's exact terminology until corrected.
- Propagate spelling corrections to active indexes and recall facts.
- Expire the stale durable fact before saving its replacement.
- Keep the original supplied screenplay or source immutable.

Report the corrected architecture first. Do not bury the change under the obsolete version or prematurely advance to the next drafting stage.

## 11. Create a resumable treatment handoff

When the author pauses a long treatment or wants to continue in a fresh session, archive one consolidated working document instead of depending on conversation recovery.

1. Preserve the approved architecture, scope exclusions, working locks, reserved mysteries, and current status at the top.
2. Use one consistent scene schema throughout: approximate runtime, environment, protagonist objective, guide/antagonist apparent objective, hidden objective, action, story function, information revealed, information withheld, and transition.
3. Complete unwritten sections only when their architecture has already been approved and the author explicitly asks for the full treatment; otherwise mark them as open rather than inventing decisions.
4. Validate the expected part count, scene count, and presence of every required field programmatically before calling the handoff complete.
5. Save the treatment under the project's installment/development area, calculate its checksum, link it from the project index, and store only the path, status, count, and checksum in durable memory.
6. Give the author one exact resume prompt naming the archived path. In the next session, load the document, summarize locked versus open material, and wait for direction before drafting screenplay pages.

Do not label the handoff as a screenplay or released canon. A durable artifact preserves work; it does not promote development status.
